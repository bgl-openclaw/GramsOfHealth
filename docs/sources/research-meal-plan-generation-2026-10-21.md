# Filter, solve, report: building a generator dietitians trust

Grams of Health should generate plans with a deterministic two-stage engine: filter the verified pool against the client's screen, assign recipes to slots variety-first with repetition and protein-spread rules, then fit servings per day as a small integer problem over quarter-serving steps, falling back to recipe profiles before declaring a residual, and always returning a plan plus a per-day, per-nutrient deviation report rather than silence. The evidence for this shape is consistent across three fields. In optimisation research, soft targets with deviation variables are feasible in 100 % of benchmark instances while hard ±5 % bands fail in 51.9 %, and rounding a continuous solution to whole servings triples the macro error, so integrality must be solved, not patched ([Aguilera Moreno 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.13849)). In the market, every dietitian tool is a filter-and-fill generator fed from a separate brief rather than the client record, none documents day-type targets, lock-and-regenerate or a practitioner-facing explanation, and the only product that puts an LLM inside plan assembly is the one most criticised for repetition and unvalidated nutrition ([Promealplan on NutriAdmin](https://www.promealplan.com/en/blog/nutriadmin-review-2026)). In practice, what dietitians and clients reject is not imprecision but impracticality: too many ingredients, unhonoured exclusions, clumped repeats and numbers that silently change after scaling ([ABC Trainerize ideas board](https://ideas.abcfitness.com/forums/167887-coach-trainer-abc-trainerize/suggestions/46973260-simpler-meal-plans); [Capterra AU, Foodzilla](https://www.capterra.com.au/reviews/206847/foodzilla)). The tolerances to adopt are daily checks on energy (±10 % accept, ±5 % stretch) and protein (never below target), with other macros and micronutrients judged on a seven-day average, mirroring NSW hospital menu standards ([NSW ACI](https://aci.health.nsw.gov.au/projects/nutrition-standards/foundations/nutrient-goals)). No professional body publishes a tolerance for written client plans, so these bands are policy anchored in the nearest evidence, not a standard. With roughly 30 verified recipes today, the generator must run in a declared degraded mode: it fills what it can, leaves slots free-choice or empty with a stated reason, and names each rule it could not enforce.

## Practicality, not nutrient precision, decides whether a plan is used

The complaints that recur across coach forums, review sites and the one peer-reviewed clinician study are about whether the client can cook and buy the plan. Coaches on the Trainerize ideas board called generated meals "overwhelming for my clients especially with the amount of ingredients required", named complexity "the biggest reason that I don't really use it", and asked for simple exclusion of a single disliked food; a client described the output as "a box of limp recipe spaghetti thrown up on a digital wall" because the first three days needed several protein powders, multiple nut milks and blue spirulina, and declared it "useless if the user can't remove ingredients due to allergies, preferences, or even cost and availability" ([Trainerize: simpler meal plans](https://ideas.abcfitness.com/forums/167887-coach-trainer-abc-trainerize/suggestions/46973260-simpler-meal-plans); [Trainerize: functional meal planner](https://ideas.abcfitness.com/forums/167887-coach-trainer-abc-trainerize/suggestions/48509438-please-make-a-functional-meal-planner)). Consumers of Real Plans reported auto-generated weeks that "clump foods together", four stews in a week, and exclusions that were "either wonky or too complicated" so excluded foods still appeared ([Trustpilot: Real Plans](https://www.trustpilot.com/review/realplans.com)). Among professionals, the sharpest complaints are numeric trust failures: a Foodzilla coach wrote that "the auto changes when altering recipe serving sizes needs to be checked before publishing to clients" and that custom-meal calories were "inconsistent", while Nutrium users complained that portion measures were "not easy for the patients" and that the database "isn't designed for people like me in Australia" ([Capterra AU: Foodzilla](https://www.capterra.com.au/reviews/206847/foodzilla); [Capterra: Nutrium](https://www.capterra.com/p/173803/Nutrium/reviews/?page=2)). When 67 clinicians rated ChatGPT weight-management plans, the limitations they flagged were conflicting clinical considerations (tomato recommended despite reflux), missing exact portion sizes, unaffordable ingredients and vagueness, and their "intention to use" score was the lowest item at **5.40/10** even though safety scored highest ([Kim et al., Frontiers in Nutrition 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fnut.2024.1374834/full)).

Two findings from clinical research push against the instinct to maximise variety. In a 12-week behavioural weight-loss programme of 112 adults, more repetitive eaters lost **5.9 % versus 4.3 %** of body weight, and each 100 kcal of daily caloric fluctuation was associated with about 0.6 % less weight loss; the authors call the result correlational ([APA release on Hagerman et al. 2026](https://apa.org/news/press/releases/2026/03/lose-weight-same-meals)). This supports deliberate repeats (same breakfast, dinner leftovers as lunch) and low between-day variance, which is also what batch cooking produces. Variety should instead be enforced where it protects adequacy: at the food-group level. The Australian Dietary Guidelines set age- and sex-specific serve targets (for example five vegetable serves and 2.5 dairy serves for women 19 to 50, rising to four dairy serves for women 51 and over), and the nutrients Australians most often miss are fibre, which only 28.2 % of adults met at the AI in 2011-12, and calcium ([Nutrition Australia ADG serves](https://nutritionaustralia.org/resources/adgs-recommended-daily-intakes); [Fayet-Moore et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5986479/)). Standard serve gram definitions were not fetched in this research and the foods table does not yet carry serve counts, so food-group scoring is a later addition, not a launch requirement.

Protein distribution is the one structural rule with a clinical anchor. The AND/DC/ACSM position recommends about 0.3 g/kg per eating occasion spread through the day for athletes, and older-adult trials that improved function used 20 to 25 g per main meal ([My Health Career summary of AND/DC/ACSM](https://www.myhealthcareer.com.au/dietetics/joint-position-statement-sport-nutritions); [Frontiers in Nutrition 2024 review](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/nutrition/articles/10.3389/fnut.2024.1394916/pdf)). For day types, the periodisation literature varies carbohydrate, not protein, and varies it meal by meal around training rather than only as a daily total ([Impey et al. 2018](https://researchonline.ljmu.ac.uk/id/eprint/8142/7/Fuel%20for%20the%20Work%20Require%20A%20Theoretical%20Framework.pdf)). Energy shares per slot (breakfast 25 to 30 %, lunch 35 to 40 % and so on) exist only as calculator convention with no clinical source and should be soft defaults. Time is the binding constraint on the dietitian side: a Medicare CDM dietetic item requires at least 20 minutes and clients are capped at five allied-health visits a year across all disciplines, and the TAC fee schedule folds "development of meal plans" into the complex consultation rather than billing it separately ([Services Australia MBS CDM rules](https://servicesaustralia.gov.au/mbs-billing-rules-for-chronic-disease-individual-allied-health-practitioner-items?context=20); [TAC dietitian fee schedule](https://www.tac.vic.gov.au/clients/how-we-can-help/treatments-and-services/payment-rates/fee-schedule/dietitians)). A reviewable draft in seconds and an approvable plan in a fraction of a consult is the economic case; no time-and-motion study exists to quantify current build time, and vendor "4-6 hours to under 30 minutes" claims come from affiliate blogs.

## Competitors filter and fill, and none binds the generator to the client record

Public documentation shows the industry converging on one architecture: hard filters (tags, exclusions, calorie range) shrink a recipe pool, then recipes are placed per slot and, in Foodzilla's case, portions are scaled once at generation. Foodzilla's Auto Scaling matches "your selected calorie target as closely as possible" but only during generation; swapping a 500 kcal breakfast for a 600 kcal recipe afterwards leaves the day at 1,600 kcal, and CSV-imported recipes "can't be used by the auto generator" ([Foodzilla: Auto-Scaling](https://foodzilla.helpscoutdocs.com/article/146-auto-scaling-in-meal-plans)). That Clean Life's Automator assumes one portion per meal, uses only its own catalogue, and warns that macro percentages give "noticeably less variety" ([TCL: Automator](https://helpdesk.thatcleanlife.com/hc/en-us/articles/49431056505243-How-Do-I-Use-Automator); [TCL: portions](https://helpdesk.thatcleanlife.com/hc/en-us/articles/49431027437083-How-Do-I-Increase-the-Number-of-Servings-or-Calories-I-Want-My-Client-to-Eat)). Eat This Much, a consumer tool, is the most completely documented: it fills "whatever nutrition a day still needs", lets subscribers set different profiles per weekday, models leftovers as source and consuming meals, avoids consecutive-day repeats once the pool exceeds about ten recipes, and admits the weekly generator misses macros because it "prioritizes a manageable grocery list" ([Eat This Much FAQ](https://www.eatthismuch.com/faq); [ETM: not hitting macros](https://help.eatthismuch.com/help/the-generator-isnt-hitting-my-target-macros-whats-going-on); [ETM: leftovers](https://help.eatthismuch.com/help/how-do-i-automate-leftovers)). Its early dev blog describes locking a food and randomising around it with a loop that "breaks after a certain number of cycles", which is the signature of a randomised greedy with an iteration cap, not an exact solver ([ETM devblog](https://devblog.eatthismuch.com/tagged/JustMigrated)). FoodWorks, the dominant Australian analysis tool, has no generator at all, and Nutrium ships fixed-energy templates at a single 20/30/50 split ([FoodWorks for dietitians](https://foodworks.online/for-dietitians); [Nutrium templates](https://wp.nutrium.com/streamline-your-appointments-with-our-meal-plans-templates/)).

Vendor speed claims ("in seconds", "up to 10x faster") are unbenchmarked, and no independent timing of any generator exists; the one reviewer who called Foodzilla "very slow" gave no numbers. A sub-two-second target for a seven-day plan is therefore a product decision, not a competitive benchmark.

## Goal programming over a shortlist is the proven accuracy engine

The formal result that should shape the servings stage comes from a 2026 mixed-integer goal-programming study. Making serving counts integer decision variables with hard bounds and treating energy and macro targets as soft goals with over- and under-deviation variables yields a model that is always feasible, with a **median maximum macro deviation of 6.3 %** and 75 % of runs inside 5 % on every macro, while the hard ±5 % band formulation found no solution in 51.9 % of instances and the common "solve continuously then round" shortcut produced a median maximum deviation of 21.6 % with no run inside 5 % ([Aguilera Moreno, arXiv 2605.13849](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.13849)). The same paper measured HiGHS solving a 15-food meal in a median 47 ms and a 25-food meal in 1.1 s, and shows the deviation variables themselves are the report: the solver "always returns a solution with a per-macro deviation report" where a hard model "returns nothing when it fails" ([Aguilera Moreno 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.13849)). Gerdessen and de Vries add the achievement-function lesson: minimising the sum of deviations can dump all the error on one nutrient, minimising the worst deviation spreads it evenly, and a blend of both is preferred ([Gerdessen & de Vries, EJCN 2015](https://www.nature.com/articles/ejcn201556)). The structural rules a planner needs are equally well established in the recipe-slot literature: Martos-Barrachina et al. cap each recipe at three appearances over a 15-day plan, forbid fish and meat at both lunch and dinner on the same day, hold daily energy within ±25 % and treat the whole thing as a feasibility problem solved by GRASP and local search, at about four seconds per seed ([Martos-Barrachina et al. 2022](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12351-022-00702-4)). Seljak's NSGA-II system repairs infeasible menus by greedy replacement of critical meals followed by simplex refinement of quantities, constrains meal energy to ±10 % and portions to 0.25x to 2x, and still needed minutes to hours per 21-day front ([Seljak 2009](https://www.dcs.gla.ac.uk/~pat/jchoco/dietProblem/papers/menu2009.pdf)). Population metaheuristics are stochastic and slow, report hypervolume rather than nutrient accuracy, and are a poor fit for an interactive, deterministic product.

The LLM evidence is unambiguous about where language models belong. In the ICML 2026 agentic optimisation work, letting an LLM interpret the profile but delegating meal selection and quantities to a numeric optimiser raised within-10-kcal adherence from **9.0 % to 79.5 %**, and a deterministic template-plus-optimiser baseline with no LLM at all was "numerically stronger on several bins" ([Soman, ICML 2026](https://icml.cc/virtual/2026/82530)). Chatbot plans evaluated against dietitian references were a mean 695 kcal low for adolescents, and Gemini missed a 1,400 to 1,800 kcal target by more than 20 % in over half its plans ([Bilen et al. 2026](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/nutrition/articles/10.3389/fnut.2026.1765598/abstract); [Kaçar et al. 2025](https://nottingham-repository.worktribe.com/index.php/output/44824313/diet-quality-and-caloric-accuracy-in-ai-generated-diet-plans-a-comparative-study-across-chatbots)). This matches the existing principle that Claude translates and explains but never places or computes.

For Grams of Health the practical consequence is that the full plan does not need a general MIP. With one recipe per slot, three to six slots per day and servings restricted to 0.25 steps inside a bound such as 0.5x to 2x, each day's serving fit has seven values per slot, which is 2,401 combinations for four slots and 16,807 for five. Exhaustive enumeration in TypeScript is deterministic, dependency-free, solves integrality exactly (so avoids the rounding failure) and runs in milliseconds; a MIP library becomes necessary only for multi-recipe slots or more than about six slots. If that day comes, the `highs` npm package (HiGHS compiled to WebAssembly, MIT, TypeScript declarations) is the right choice over GPL-licensed `glpk.js` or the weaker pure-JS `javascript-lp-solver` ([npm: highs](https://npmjs.com/package/highs); [glpk.js](https://www.npmjs.com/package/glpk.js); [jsLPSolver README](https://cdn.jsdelivr.net/npm/javascript-lp-solver@1.0.3/README.md)). No benchmark of the wasm build exists, and there is no citable source for the 0.5x to 2x bound or quarter-serving rounding as conventions; Seljak's 0.25x to 2x portion bound is the nearest published figure. Determinism needs three disciplines the literature implies but never states: tie-break every score on a stable key (recipe id), apply a seeded PRNG (mulberry32 or xorshift) only to candidate shuffling, and never use wall-clock limits, since timeouts make results depend on machine speed.

## Tolerances: energy and protein every day, everything else over the week

No professional body or peer-reviewed source states an acceptable deviation band for a written client plan; this research found none for Australia or elsewhere. The bands below are therefore policy choices anchored to the nearest evidence, and the report should present them to dietitians as such.

Energy shares per slot should remain soft defaults the dietitian can edit; they have no clinical source. Weight-loss protein targets (often quoted as 1.2 to 1.6 g/kg) and carbohydrate g/kg bands by training load were not verifiable in this research and should be set by the dietitian in the profile, which is already the design.

## Recommended design: the generator as ten deterministic steps

The generator is a pure function `generate(profile, poolSnapshot, settings, seed) -> { plan, report }`. It reads per-serving nutrient vectors that the macro engine has already computed for each recipe and profile variant, treats them as constants, and after assembly hands the plan to the P2 roll-up for the authoritative totals. It never computes nutrition itself; if the roll-up disagrees with the generator's working totals by more than rounding, that is a bug and the test suite says so.

1. **Resolve settings.** Expand the weekly day-type pattern into per-day targets (energy, protein, carbohydrate, fat, fibre) for the cycle length. Apply the dietitian's slot list, energy-share defaults per slot (soft), free-choice slots, repetition rules, batch-cooking flag, protein spread counts and tolerances. Record the resolved settings and seed in the plan's origin so a rerun is reproducible.
2. **Build the pool.** Start from recipes with `verified = true` and complete nutrient coverage for the tracked nutrients; a recipe whose energy or protein depends on an unverified or imputed food is excluded, following the food-composition rule that missing values are never treated as zero ([EuroFIR imputation procedures](https://www.eurofir.org/wp-admin/wp-content/uploads/2019/03/D2.2.13-Procedures-for-imputing-component-values-in-FCDBs_PUBLIC.pdf)). Run the client screen: `blocked` and `unconfirmed` recipes are removed (allergen tags are facts, the screen decides), `disliked` recipes carry a penalty, `ok` pass. Partition by meal-type tag per slot; recipes without a tag go to no slot and are counted.
3. **Pre-check and declare mode.** For each slot, count eligible recipes and compute the maximum and minimum achievable energy and protein from the pool at serving bounds. If a target exceeds the maximum achievable, report it before solving, in the form "Cannot reach protein target of 140 g on hard days. Maximum achievable from 6 eligible dinners: 112 g", the pattern the MIGP pre-check uses ([Aguilera Moreno 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.13849)). If any slot has fewer eligible recipes than the repetition rule needs across the cycle, mark the plan `degraded` and state which rule will be relaxed. If protein families are set on fewer than, say, 80 % of the pool, mark the protein spread as unenforceable and say so rather than enforcing it on a biased subset.
4. **Place anchors.** Lock slots the dietitian has pinned. Place free-choice slots as `free_choice` with their energy and protein allowance subtracted from the day's remaining targets. If batch cooking is on, pre-assign leftover chains: a cooked recipe on day d feeds the linked slot on day d+1 (or the pattern the client chose), with cooked servings equal to the sum of consuming servings; this follows the only shipped model that treats leftovers as a generation constraint ([ETM: leftovers](https://help.eatthismuch.com/help/how-do-i-automate-leftovers)).
5. **Assign recipes, variety first, by beam search.** Walk days in order and slots in descending energy share. For each open slot, score every eligible candidate: nominal-serving fit to the slot's remaining energy and protein allowance (normalised deviation), repetition penalty against a look-back window and the per-cycle cap, protein-family penalty when the family's count for the cycle is already met, dislike penalty, batch-cooking bonus for recipes with a leftover chain, and a small favourite bonus. Shuffle ties with the seeded PRNG, then break remaining ties by recipe id. Keep the top k (default 4) per slot and a beam of width B (default 8) across the day so a poor first pick cannot lock in a poor day, the failure the household-planner spec and the greedy literature warn about ([Meal-Planner design spec](https://git.research.bike/admin/Meal-Planner/src/branch/main/docs/specs/2026-05-05-meal-planner-algorithm-design.md)). Carry the repetition and protein-family state across days.
6. **Fit servings per day by exact enumeration.** For each beam candidate day, enumerate servings in 0.25 steps within bounds for each filled slot and choose the combination minimising a blended achievement function: the largest normalised deviation across energy, protein, carbohydrate and fat (MinMax), plus 0.1 times their sum (MinSum), with protein under-shoot weighted double so error does not land on the nutrient that matters daily. This is the extended goal-programming recommendation applied to a problem small enough to solve by brute force ([Gerdessen & de Vries 2015](https://www.nature.com/articles/ejcn201556)). Pick the beam member with the best fitted score. Prefer servings near 1.0 when scores tie, since Eat This Much users complain about large portions produced by narrow pools ([ETM: multiple servings](https://help.eatthismuch.com/help/why-are-there-multiple-servings-can-i-force-the-generator-to-use-one-serving-per-meal)).
7. **Fall back to profiles.** If a day is still outside the accept band on any daily quantity, try swapping one slot at a time to an existing recipe profile (High protein, Low fat, Higher fibre, Lighter) and re-run step 6, keeping the change only if the score improves. Profiles are already computed by the macro engine, so this stays within the no-compute rule.
8. **Repair.** For any day still outside tolerance, replace the worst-fitting unlocked slot with the next candidate from step 5 and re-fit, up to a fixed iteration count (not a time limit). Stop when within tolerance or when the count is exhausted. Leave a slot `empty` with a reason rather than fill it with a blocked, disliked or badly fitting recipe.
9. **Roll up and verify.** Call the P2 roll-up for every day and the cycle. Compare against the tolerance table at the correct cadence (daily for energy and protein, weekly average for the rest). Compute the between-day energy standard deviation per day type, distinct recipes, distinct ingredients and cook events per cycle.
10. **Score and explain.** Emit the report: per day and per nutrient the signed deviation and band status; each rule met, relaxed or unenforceable with the reason and the count that caused it ("Lunch pool after screen: 4 recipes; repetition rule 'no repeat within 3 days' relaxed to 2 days on days 4 and 6"); the pre-check limits; servings outside 0.75x to 1.5x flagged for the dietitian's eye; and up to three relaxation suggestions ranked by how many days each would bring into tolerance, never applied automatically. Persist plan, report, resolved settings and seed.

**Infeasibility and explanation behaviour.** The generator never returns an empty result. Three outcomes exist: a plan within tolerance; a plan with a report naming every miss; or, when a slot has zero eligible recipes, a plan with that slot `empty` and the report stating the cause. Hard constraints are only safety and structure (blocked, unconfirmed, locked slots, serving bounds, one recipe per filled slot). Everything else is soft and reported. Relaxation is suggested, not chosen, because practice leaves that decision to a human and no study has evaluated automatic relaxation ([Koenen et al. 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/nutrition/articles/10.3389/fnut.2022.1056205/pdf)). The report is the primary input for the Claude explainer in P5, which renders it in two registers: a dietitian view that lists rules and numbers, and a client view that says why the week looks the way it does and what to do when life intervenes. The explainer reads the report; it does not re-derive it.

**Editing operations.** Every operation takes a plan and returns a diff plus a fresh report, is logged, and defaults to re-fitting the affected day's servings, the inverse of Foodzilla's documented behaviour that leaves a swapped day 100 kcal off ([Foodzilla: Auto-Scaling](https://foodzilla.helpscoutdocs.com/article/146-auto-scaling-in-meal-plans)). `swapInSlot(day, slot, recipeId?)` offers alternatives ranked by fitted-day score and variety impact, applies the chosen one and re-runs steps 6 to 10 for that day; `swapEverywhere` extends it to all instances of a recipe, as TCL's "Swap All" does. `nudgePlan(delta)` adjusts energy or protein targets by a percentage or absolute amount and re-runs steps 6 to 10 across the cycle without changing recipes unless no serving fit exists, in which case it reports which days need a swap. `fixDay(day)` re-runs steps 5 to 10 for one day holding locked slots. `applyDislike(ingredientOrRecipe)` writes the dislike to the profile, removes every affected slot and refills them with steps 5 to 8, reporting the variety cost. `lock`/`unlock`, `moveSlot(from, to)`, `markLeftover(source, target)` (creates the link and sets cooked servings), `setFreeChoice`, `regenerate(seed+1)` (new variant, locks held) and `regenerateExcluding(recipeIds)` (deterministic, no new seed) complete the set. Lock-and-regenerate and nudge were not requested by name in any review, so they are hypotheses to validate with the practice's dietitians, but swap and move are the two actions practitioners praise most ([Stephanie Long TCL review](https://www.stephanielong.ca/blog/that-clean-life-review-meal-planning-software); [Capterra AU: Foodzilla](https://www.capterra.com.au/reviews/206847/foodzilla)).

## Testing and acceptance criteria that close the gaps the literature leaves open

Published generator evaluations rarely test the things dietitians care about most. Allergen adherence is almost never measured, drug-food violation rates of 11 to 17 % were seen even in frontier models, and models that named no specific foods scored zero violations while being useless ([NutriOrion, arXiv 2602.18650](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.18650); [NutriGen, arXiv 2502.20601](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.20601)). The test suite should therefore treat safety as an invariant, not a score, and pair every pass rate with a specificity check.

| Test class | Criterion | Anchor |
|---|---|---|
| Determinism | Same profile, pool snapshot, settings and seed produce a byte-identical plan and report, across runtimes (Node, Deno edge function, browser) | MILP results are deterministic for a fixed model; metaheuristics report over 30 seeds ([Ramos-Pérez et al. 2021](https://www.mdpi.com/2227-7390/9/1/80)) |
| Safety invariant | For any generated pool and any screen, no `blocked` or `unconfirmed` recipe appears in any slot, including leftovers and after every edit operation; zero tolerance | Gap in literature; [NutriOrion](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.18650) violation rates |
| Never empty | Every call returns a plan plus report; an `empty` slot always carries a reason; no exception path returns nothing | Hard-IP returned nothing in 51.9 % of instances ([Aguilera Moreno 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.13849)) |
| Every miss reported | For every day and nutrient, either within band or listed in the report with the correct signed deviation; property-based test over random profiles | Deviation variables as report ([Aguilera Moreno 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.13849)) |
| Totals integrity | Generator working totals equal P2 roll-up totals for every plan; roll-up totals equal an independent recomputation from the foods table | Arithmetic failures in chatbot plans ([NutriGen](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.20601)) |
| Data completeness | A plan never includes a recipe whose tracked nutrients depend on a missing or imputed value without the report saying so | [EuroFIR](https://www.eurofir.org/wp-admin/wp-content/uploads/2019/03/D2.2.13-Procedures-for-imputing-component-values-in-FCDBs_PUBLIC.pdf) |
| Servings | All servings are multiples of 0.25 within bounds; leftover consumed servings sum to cooked servings | Policy; Seljak 0.25x to 2x |
| Variety rules | Repetition and protein-spread rules hold, or the report names the relaxation and the pool count that forced it | Frequency-checklist practice ([WA Health MOW](https://fsfhg.health.wa.gov.au/~/media/Files/Corporate/general-documents/HACC/PDF/8631_Menu_planning_MOW.pdf)) |
| Edit operations | Each operation changes only the slots it names (plus re-fitted servings on affected days), preserves locks, and the post-edit report is regenerated | Foodzilla no-rescale complaint ([Capterra AU](https://www.capterra.com.au/reviews/206847/foodzilla)) |
| Golden plans | Five to ten frozen personas (including coeliac, vegan no soy, low FODMAP, older adult, athlete with hard/moderate/rest pattern, batch cooker) against a frozen pool snapshot; any diff fails CI until a dietitian re-approves | Persona fixtures in [NextNutri](https://www.researchsquare.com/article/rs-10232285) and the 270-instance grid in [MIGP](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.13849) |
| Benchmark grid | Pool size 30/100/300 x energy 6,000/9,000/13,000 kJ x slots 3/4/5 x tightness; report feasibility rate, share of days within accept and stretch bands, median and p95 run time | [MIGP](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.13849) harness |
| Performance | Seven-day, five-slot plan from a 300-recipe pool in under 2 s p95 on the edge function; under 200 ms for any single edit operation | Product decision; no public benchmark exists |
| Dietitian evaluation | Two or more blinded dietitians per plan, 5-point Likert on adequacy, safety, realism and "would you give this to this client", with agreement reported; go/no-go at 70 % sign-off without edits beyond swaps and servings | 64.4 % sign-off and κ 0.82 in [NutriOrion](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.18650); 5.40/10 intention to use in [Kim et al. 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fnut.2024.1374834/full) |
| Practicality metrics | Distinct ingredients, distinct recipes and cook events per cycle reported on every plan; thresholds set by the practice after the first 20 real plans | Not found as literature metrics; product metrics |

Acceptance for P3 on the current 30-recipe pool should be stated honestly: 100 % on determinism, safety, never-empty, every-miss-reported and totals integrity; a share of days within the ±10 % energy band that is measured and published on the pool-health screen rather than promised; and a report that correctly names every rule it could not enforce. Accuracy targets (for example 90 % of days within ±10 % energy and 100 % at or above protein) become acceptance criteria only once the pool passes about 100 verified, meal-type-tagged recipes, because the MIGP evidence shows accuracy is a function of pool size: median maximum deviation fell from 6.3 % overall to 1.4 % at 15 foods and 0.3 % at 25 foods in non-ambitious configurations ([Aguilera Moreno 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.13849)).

## Changes to make to P1 to P6

**P0 data and safety.** Meal-type tags and protein families are now generator blockers, not nice-to-haves: only about 72 of 374 recipes carry a meal type and families are mostly unset, so step 3 will declare degraded mode on nearly every run until tagging is done. Add a `nutrient_completeness` flag per recipe derived from the foods table (any tracked nutrient missing or imputed on any ingredient), and a `discretionary` tag. Record the NRV set per client age, sex and life stage once the iron, calcium, B12, iodine and sodium values have been verified from eatforhealth.gov.au.

**P1 plan model.** Keep numeric servings in 0.25 steps, recipe or food items, slot states and leftover links, and add: a `locked` flag per slot; a `reason` on `empty` and `free_choice` slots; a `profile_variant` reference when a profile replaced the base recipe; a stored `report` JSON alongside `status`, `origin` and `seed`; the resolved settings snapshot and pool snapshot id used to generate; a `batch_group` id linking the cooked item and its leftovers; and per-slot visible energy and protein so the client sees the numbers the dietitian checked, which Nutrium users asked for ([Capterra: Nutrium](https://www.capterra.com/p/173803/Nutrium/reviews/)). Add a plan edit log table (operation, actor, diff, timestamp) so dietitians can see what they changed.

**P2 roll-up.** Beyond totals, return the comparison object the report needs: per-day deviations for energy, protein, carbohydrate and fat against the day-type target; weekly averages for fibre, saturated fat and micronutrients against the client's NRV set with EAR/RDI/UL banding; between-day energy standard deviation per day type; distinct recipes, ingredients and cook events; and a `confidence` flag that is false whenever any included item lacks complete nutrient data. Food-group serve counts go here later, once serve definitions exist on the foods table.

**P3 deterministic generator.** Adopt the ten steps above. Specifically: add the pre-check (step 3) and degraded-mode declaration before any assignment; use beam search rather than pure greedy for slot assignment; fit servings by exact enumeration with the blended MinMax/MinSum objective rather than pro-rata scaling then rounding; use an iteration cap, not a time limit, in repair; make the report a first-class output with ranked, unapplied relaxation suggestions. Keep the existing adjust solver for ingredient-level kitchen rounding inside a recipe; the generator only chooses serving multipliers.

**P4 edit operations.** Add `lock`/`unlock`, `moveSlot`, `markLeftover`, `setFreeChoice`, `swapEverywhere`, `regenerate(seed)` and `regenerateExcluding` to the planned `swapInSlot`, `nudgePlan`, `fixDay` and `applyDislike`. Make "re-fit the day's servings" the default on every operation with a visible toggle, and return a diff plus the regenerated report from every call. `swapInSlot` must present ranked alternatives, not a single replacement.

**P5 AI edge function.** The translator converts a dietitian's free-text brief into the settings object and must show the parsed settings for confirmation before any generation runs, the pattern Foodzilla documents ([Foodzilla: AI text brief](https://foodzilla.helpscoutdocs.com/article/223-how-to-use-ai-to-generate-a-meal-plan-from-a-text-description)). The strategist proposes edit operations as diffs (never direct writes) in response to requests such as "make Tuesday lighter and move the salmon to Thursday", and the dietitian approves. The explainer renders the P3 report in dietitian and client registers and must not restate numbers that are not in the report. Refusals (clinical questions outside scope, requests to override a blocked allergen) are logged with the prompt and the reason. Every call is logged with plan id, report hash and model version so an explanation can be traced to the exact plan state.

**P6 Lovable screens.** Before generation: a pool-health panel showing eligible recipes per slot after the screen, the maximum achievable energy and protein, and the rules that will be degraded, modelled on TCL's live "Recipes matching all rules" counter but per slot. After generation: the plan grid with per-day totals against the day-type target (per day, not weekly average, which a Foodzilla reviewer explicitly asked for), the satisfaction report as a checklist of met, relaxed and infeasible rules, the swap picker with ranked alternatives and their effect on the day, and the edit log. For clients: a simple view that defaults to meal names, portions in Australian household measures and a shopping list with pantry check-off, with numbers available but not foregrounded, because older and less technical clients find dense apps overwhelming ([Capterra AU: Foodzilla](https://www.capterra.com.au/reviews/206847/foodzilla); [Trustpilot: Real Plans](https://www.trustpilot.com/review/realplans.com)). A "framework" output (a sample day plus swap lists) should be considered for clients whose dietitian does not want a seven-day prescription, since a visible minority of dietitians avoid prescriptive plans except for athletes and clinical cases ([Delish Knowledge](https://www.delishknowledge.com/why-meal-plans-arent-the-answer)).

## Conclusion

The research reframes the generator's job. The hard, well-evidenced part, fitting servings to targets, is small enough to solve exactly in TypeScript without a solver dependency once recipes are chosen, and the evidence says to solve it exactly rather than scale and round. The part that decides adoption, whether a dietitian signs off in minutes and a client cooks it, depends on the pool, the screen, the repetition design and the honesty of the report, none of which an algorithm can supply. That is why the single highest-leverage investment is not in P3 but in P0: every verified, meal-type-tagged recipe with a protein family both raises accuracy and shrinks the list of rules the report has to apologise for.

Two choices distinguish this design from the market. First, the generator is bound to the client record and to day types, so the plan a dietitian reviews already reflects the profile they built in the consult, where every competitor re-keys a brief. Second, the report is the product's trust mechanism: by naming each relaxation with the pool count that caused it, it converts the generator's limitations into a work queue for the recipe library and gives dietitians the pre-send checklist they already run by hand. The acceptance criteria should be read the same way; determinism, safety and honest reporting are pass-fail from day one, while accuracy bands are measured publicly and promoted to criteria only as the verified pool grows.
